  • Average miss is how far off the forecast was, as a percent of the price, averaged over all the forecasts.
  • Better than benchmark by is how much smaller that average miss was than the benchmark's. A minus means it was bigger.
  • Nearer than benchmark and right direction are shares of the {{ res.forecasts }} forecasts: how often the method was nearer to the real price, and how often it called up or down correctly.
  • Luck test is the chance of a gap this big if the method were really no better than the benchmark, after allowing for trying {{ res.rows|length - 1 }} methods. Under 0.05 counts as real.

How to read this

  • Every forecast only used prices up to the day it was made. Nothing from after that day leaks in, and the methods that learn from the past only learn from outcomes that were already known by then.
  • The {{ res.forecasts }} forecasts are {{ res.every }} trading days apart. Forecasts closer than {{ res.h }} days share some of the same prices, so the luck test counts them as fewer than {{ res.forecasts }} separate tests.
  • The luck test allows for the {{ res.rows|length - 1 }} methods tried here. It cannot allow for how many stocks and horizons you try: look at 20 and about one will show a winner by luck alone.
  • Beating "price stays put" is hard. Prices already reflect what is known, so most methods land within luck of it. That is the usual result, not a fault in the test.
  • A method that wins on one stock or one stretch of years may not win on another, or tomorrow. This looks backwards. It is not a prediction and not financial advice.
What the {{ res.rows|length|words }} methods are
  • Price stays put: today's price is the forecast. The benchmark everything is marked against.
  • Drift: keeps growing at the average pace of the last year.
  • Damped trend (Holt): exponential smoothing with a trend that fades out, fitted on the last 2 years.
  • ARIMA(1,1,1): a classic time series model with a steady drift, fitted on the last 2 years.
  • Prophet (trend only): Meta's forecasting library, fitted on the last 2 years. It uses its trend alone: the prices carry no dates, so it has no weekly or yearly pattern to find.
  • Momentum regression: a ridge regression on recent returns, recent volatility and how far the price is from its 200 day average.
  • Gradient boosting: a small set of decision trees on the same inputs.
